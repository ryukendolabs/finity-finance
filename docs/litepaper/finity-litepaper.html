Finity Finance · LitepaperThe weekend problem

1 · The weekend problem

Tokenized stocks trade on-chain every hour of the week. The stocks behind them do not.

A US equity trades in a regular session of 6.5 hours, five days a week. Out of the 168 hours in a week, the reference market is open for

$$\frac{6.5 \times 5}{168} = \frac{32.5}{168} \approx 19.3\%$$

of the time. For the other 80.7%, including every overnight, every weekend and every holiday, the token keeps trading while the price that gives it value is frozen. Any on-chain price in that window is a guess made in a thin market, and the true price is only revealed at the reopen, as a gap:

$$G \;=\; \frac{P_{\text{open}}}{P_{\text{close}}} - 1$$

A lender that marks collateral continuously faces two bad outcomes. If it trusts the weekend price, it liquidates borrowers on moves that the reopen may erase. If it ignores the weekend, it carries the gap: a loan with debt \(D\) against collateral worth \(V\) at the close is short of collateral at the reopen exactly when

$$D \;>\; V\,(1+G) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad G \;<\; \frac{D}{V} - 1 \;=\; \text{LTV} - 1 .$$

At 75% LTV the lender loses money only if the stock gaps down by more than 25%, which is rare but not impossible: single-name weekend gaps of that size follow earnings, fraud findings and macro shocks. The question is not whether to lend through closures but how much, and that depends on how long the closure is and how volatile that stock is.

Finity's principle

Every rule follows the asset's own clock. While the market is open, loans are marked to a live price. As a closure approaches, new borrowing is capped at an LTV that history says survives the gap. While it is closed, nobody is liquidated on a price the market never printed; repaying and adding collateral always work.

What this paper covers

Sections 2 to 6 describe the full protocol: the Asset Clock, the closure-safe LTV and its statistics, the interest-rate model, the pricing of Gap Cover (insurance on the reopen) and the order in which losses are absorbed. Section 7 describes Finity Lend, the small program live on Solana mainnet today. Section 8 previews $FINO staking. All formulas are the ones the code runs; the worked examples use the protocol's calibrated parameters.

2 · The Asset Clock and the Bell

Each market carries a clock that knows its exchange calendar: sessions, early closes, holidays, halts and corporate actions.

The clock moves through six states: Regular, Extended, Closed, Reopen, Halted and Corporate action. The borrowing limit that applies at time \(t\) is chosen from the state:

$$L(t)=\begin{cases}\text{LTV}_{\max} & \text{regular and } t < t_{\text{close}}-2\,\text{h}\\[2pt] \text{LTV}_{\text{safe}}(c)\ \text{measured on } D_{\text{proj}} & \text{Bell window, extended, closed or halted}\\[2pt] 0\ \ (\text{new debt paused}) & \text{reopen or corporate action}\end{cases}$$

where \(c\) is the closure the rule looks at: the one in progress, else the next scheduled one (overnight, weekend or holiday weekend; halts use the holiday-weekend set because their length is unknown).

Projected debt

Interest keeps accruing while the market is closed, so the safe rule measures debt as it will stand at the reopen:

$$D_{\text{proj}} = D\left(1 + r\cdot\frac{d}{365}\right),\qquad d = \left\lceil \frac{t_{\text{reopen}}-t_{\text{close}}}{86\,400} \right\rceil$$

Example: \(D = \$1{,}000\) at \(r = 5.33\%\) over a 3-day weekend gives \(D_{\text{proj}} = 1000\,(1 + 0.0533\cdot 3/365) = \$1{,}000.44\).

The Bell

Two hours before the close the Bell window opens. A loan is flagged when

$$\frac{D_{\text{proj}}}{V} \;>\; \text{LTV}_{\text{safe}}(c).$$

A flagged borrower has until the Bell deadline (15 minutes before the close) to repay, add collateral or buy Gap Cover (section 5). After the deadline the Bell can auto-cover a loan that is within the coverable limit, adding the premium to its debt. Nobody is liquidated during the closure itself.

3 · Closure-safe LTV

How much can a loan borrow and still be covered after the worst gap history shows, at a chosen confidence?

For each asset and closure type the protocol stores a scenario set: \(n\) standardized historical gaps \(z_1 \le z_2 \le \dots \le z_n\), measured in units of the asset's per-closure volatility \(\sigma\). Sets are pooled across a peer group so the tail is not estimated from one name alone; AAPL's weekend set pools 16 large-cap technology stocks (7,552 observations) and applies a Student-t (3 d.o.f.) floor to the extreme tail. Each scenario maps to a gap factor, the fraction of today's collateral value that survives the reopen:

$$g(z) = \max\!\big(0,\; 1 + \sigma z - \delta\big)\,(1-\kappa)$$

with \(\delta\) a known dividend drop and \(\kappa = 3\%\) a liquidation-cost haircut. The safe LTV is the gap factor at the \(\alpha\)-quantile of the scenarios, capped at the market's maximum:

$$\text{LTV}_{\text{safe}} = \min\!\Big(g\big(z_{(k)}\big),\ \text{LTV}_{\max}\Big),\qquad k=\lceil \alpha n\rceil,\quad \alpha = 0.1\%$$

Read it as: in 99.9% of the stored scenarios, a loan at \(\text{LTV}_{\text{safe}}\) is still fully covered at the reopen after the haircut. The computation is integer fixed point on-chain (18-decimal WAD), and the scenario sets are content-hashed so anyone can check what the program uses.

Worked example: AAPL

Closure\(\sigma\)\(n\)\(k\)\(z_{(k)}\)\(g(z_{(k)})\)Safe LTV
Overnight0.946%3,0003−11.14486.78%75%
Weekend1.068%3,0003−7.20889.54%75%

For the weekend: \(g = (1 - 0.010675 \times 7.208)(1 - 0.03) = 0.92305 \times 0.97 = 0.8954\). The worst 0.1% of weekend gaps would still leave an 89.5%-LTV loan covered, so for a mega-cap like AAPL the 75% maximum binds and the closure costs the borrower nothing. A more volatile name, or a longer holiday closure, produces a smaller \(g\) and a lower limit automatically.

Deployment note

The mainnet program (section 7) does not run the scenario engine on-chain yet. It applies one conservative closure-safe limit, 55%, at all times, and lends only while a fresh price exists. The engine above runs in the full protocol.

4 · Rates and the lending pool

Each market has its own kinked interest-rate curve, driven by utilization.

Utilization is the share of supplied cash that is lent out, \(u = B/S\). The borrow rate rises gently up to a kink \(u^\ast\) and steeply after it, which pulls utilization back below the kink by raising the cost of the last dollars:

$$r(u)=\begin{cases} r_0 + s_1\,\dfrac{u}{u^\ast} & u \le u^\ast\\[8pt] r_0 + s_1 + s_2\,\dfrac{u-u^\ast}{1-u^\ast} & u > u^\ast\end{cases}$$

Accrual and the fee split

Interest accrues per second on total borrows \(B\) over the elapsed time \(\Delta t\) (\(Y\) = seconds per year), and is split three ways: 80% to senior lenders, 10% to the Underwriter Pool, 10% to the protocol reserve.

$$i = B\, r(u)\,\frac{\Delta t}{Y},\qquad \text{APY}_{\text{supply}} = r(u)\cdot u\cdot(1-f_{\text{pool}}-f_{\text{reserve}}) = 0.8\,u\,r(u)$$

Vault shares

Lenders hold shares of one senior vault. Share price uses virtual shares \(S_0 = 1{,}000\) and one virtual asset, so the first depositor cannot inflate the price to steal later deposits:

$$\text{assets}(s) = \frac{s\,(A+1)}{S + S_0},\qquad \text{shares}(a)=\frac{a\,(S+S_0)}{A+1}$$

5 · Pricing Gap Cover

A borrower who wants to stay above the safe LTV through a closure can buy Gap Cover: if the reopen gaps past the collateral, the Underwriter Pool pays the shortfall.

The premium is priced from the same scenario set. For each scenario the loss to the lender is the part of the projected debt that the surviving collateral does not cover:

$$L_i = \max\!\big(0,\; D_{\text{proj}} - V\,g(z_i)\big)$$

From the losses the protocol takes the expected loss and the expected shortfall in the worst \(1-\beta\) of scenarios:

$$\text{EL} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} L_i,\qquad \text{ES}_\beta = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} L_{(i)},\qquad m=\lceil (1-\beta)\,n\rceil$$

The premium pays for the expected loss with a safety loading \(\theta\), plus the cost of holding capital against the tail for the length of the closure \(\tau = d/365\), and rises with the pool's utilization after the sale \(u'\):

$$\pi = \max\!\Big(\pi_{\min},\ \big(1+\eta\,u'^2\big)\big[(1+\theta)\,\text{EL} + c\,\tau\,\text{ES}_\beta\big]\Big)$$

Worked example · AAPL, weekend

Collateral \(V = \$10{,}000\), projected debt \(D_{\text{proj}} = \$9{,}000\) (90% LTV, far above the 75% limit), 3-day closure, pool utilization after the sale \(u' = 30\%\). Of the 3,000 scenarios only three lose money: \(L = 418.55,\ 131.30,\ 46.39\).

\(\text{EL} = 596.24/3000 = 0.1988\),  \(m = \lceil 0.025\times3000\rceil = 75\),  \(\text{ES} = 596.24/75 = 7.950\),  \(\tau = 3/365\)
\(\pi = (1+4\times0.09)\,[\,2\times0.1988 + 0.15\times\tfrac{3}{365}\times7.950\,] = 1.36\times0.4073 = \mathbf{0.554\ USDC}\)

Keeping a 90% LTV through the weekend costs about 55 cents on $9,000 of debt, because a gap large enough to hurt happened in 0.1% of the history. The same formula prices a volatile name or a long holiday much higher, and the \(\eta u'^2\) term makes cover expensive before the pool runs out of capacity.

6 · Who pays first; liquidations

Losses are absorbed in a fixed order, so senior lenders know exactly what stands in front of them.

For a shortfall \(S\) at a reopen, with pool net asset value \(N_p\) and reserve cash \(R\):

$$S_{\text{pool}} = \min(S, N_p),\quad S_{\text{reserve}} = \min(S - S_{\text{pool}},\, R),\quad S_{\text{senior}} = S - S_{\text{pool}} - S_{\text{reserve}}$$

The pool's capacity limits how much cover it may sell: it holds a stress buffer computed over \(K = 256\) joint scenarios (all markets gapping together), and stops selling at \(u_{\max} = 50\%\) utilization, so one bad weekend cannot empty it.

Health and liquidation (open market only)

While the market trades, a loan is marked to the live price \(P\). With liquidation threshold \(\text{LT}\) (80% for stocks):

$$\text{HF} = \frac{V\cdot \text{LT}}{D},\qquad \text{liquidatable} \iff \text{HF} < 1$$

A keeper may repay up to half the debt (close factor 50%) and receives collateral worth that repayment plus a bonus \(b\) (3%):

$$\text{seized} = \frac{R\,(1+b)}{P},\qquad R \le 0.5\,D$$

Example

10 AAPL at $200 (\(V = \$2{,}000\)), debt \(D = \$1{,}650\): \(\text{HF} = 2000\times0.8/1650 = 0.970 < 1\). A keeper repays \(R = \$825\) and seizes \(825\times1.03/200 = 4.249\) AAPL. The loan is left with \(D = \$825\) against 5.751 AAPL (\(V = \$1{,}150\)): \(\text{HF} = 1150\times0.8/825 = 1.115\), healthy again.

During a closure there is no liquidation at all: the Bell, the safe LTV and Gap Cover replace it, and the loan is marked again from the first price after the reopen.

7 · Finity Lend on mainnet

The first live step: borrow USDC against xStocks on Solana mainnet, in a 9,384-byte program.

Finity Lend lists six xStocks (AAPLx, AMZNx, GOOGLx, MSFTx, NVDAx, TSLAx; Token-2022, 8 decimals) against one USDC pool. A borrower deposits collateral and borrows in one instruction, and gets all the collateral back in the instruction that repays the loan. The program is written in Pinocchio (no framework), so it fits a deploy budget of under $5.

The limit check, without division

Prices and loans carry 6 decimals. The program checks the limit in 128-bit integers with no division (division is an expensive software routine on Solana's virtual machine):

$$D \cdot 10^{4}\cdot 10^{6} \;\le\; C \cdot P \cdot \text{LTV}_{\text{bps}}$$

where \(C\) is collateral in base units and \(P\) is the posted price per \(10^6\) base units. For a token with \(e\) decimals the keeper posts \(P = p\cdot10^{6}/10^{e}\), i.e. \(p/100\) for the 8-decimal xStocks, so the same check serves every token. The left side stays below \(2^{98}\); the right side is bounded before multiplying, so nothing overflows.

Worked example · NVDAx (verified on-chain)

5 NVDAx \(= 5\times10^{8}\) units at $156.14: \(P = 156.14\times10^{6}/100 = 1{,}561{,}400\). Maximum debt \(= \lfloor 5\cdot10^{8}\times1{,}561{,}400\times5{,}500 / 10^{10}\rfloor = 429{,}385{,}000\) units \(= \mathbf{429.385}\) USDC, exactly 55% of $780.70. One more unit is refused by the program.

Freshness and pausing

A keeper posts all six prices in one transaction every 15 minutes while US markets are open. Borrowing needs

$$t_{\text{now}} - t_{\text{price}} \le 1{,}200\ \text{s} \quad\text{and}\quad P > 0,$$

so outside market hours, or if the keeper stops, new loans simply wait. Repaying never needs a price.

8 · $FINO staking PREVIEW

Lock $FINO for a fixed term and receive the principal plus a fixed reward at unlock. Staking opens with the token; the rates below are illustrative.

A stake of \(A\) tokens locked for term \(T\) at reward rate \(r_T\) pays, automatically at the unlock time \(t_0 + T\):

$$\text{payout} = A\,(1 + r_T),\qquad \text{annualized rate} = r_T\cdot\frac{365}{T}$$

Longer terms earn a higher annualized rate, which rewards commitment. Because rewards are fixed at lock time, the program must always be able to pay every open ticket. With \(j\) open stakes and a reward reserve \(Q\):

$$\sum_{j} A_j\, r_{T_j} \;\le\; Q \qquad\text{(checked before every new lock)}$$

A lock that would break this inequality is refused, so the staking vault is solvent by construction: it holds every principal plus every promised reward.

Lower loan fees

A wallet with an active lock pays a lower fee on Finity loans. For a fee \(f\) on a loan of size \(D\), the saving over a year of borrowing is \((f_{\text{base}} - f_{\text{staked}})\,D\); for an active borrower this can exceed the staking reward itself.

Status

The staking page is live as a preview: it shows the terms and computes payouts, but no tokens move until $FINO launches. Terms, rates, the reserve \(Q\) and the fee discount will be fixed and published before launch.

9 · Risks and roadmap

What can go wrong, and what comes next.

Risks

  • No liquidation on mainnet yet. Finity Lend checks the limit only when borrowing. If a stock falls far enough that \(D > V\), a borrower can keep the USDC and the pool takes the loss. The 55% limit means a fall of more than \(1 - 0.55 = 45\%\) before that happens; the pool is kept small until liquidation ships.
  • Price authority. Prices are posted by one keeper key. Whoever controls it could post a wrong price; key security and, later, a multi-source oracle address this.
  • Issuer controls. xStocks carry a permanent delegate and a pause switch held by their issuer, who can move or freeze tokens, including those in Finity's vaults.
  • Model risk. The safe LTV is only as good as the scenario sets. History can underestimate a new kind of shock; the haircut \(\kappa\), the t-tail floor and the cap \(\text{LTV}_{\max}\) are the margins of safety.
  • Smart-contract risk. The programs are tested (end-to-end against the real AAPLx mint) but not yet externally audited.

Summary

Finity lends against tokenized stocks on the stocks' own schedule. The maths is simple to state: lend \(\min(g(z_{(\lceil \alpha n \rceil)}),\ \text{LTV}_{\max})\) through a closure, price the rest as insurance, and never liquidate on a price the market never printed.
